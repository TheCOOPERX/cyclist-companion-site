How pressure is calculated

No black box. Here is the whole model, including the parts where it stops being reliable.

The principle: constant tyre drop

A loaded tyre flattens slightly where it meets the road. The amount it flattens, as a proportion of its height, is called tyre drop. Decades of measurement suggest that a drop of roughly 15% is the sweet spot: enough deflection for the casing to absorb road texture, not so much that the tyre squirms, rolls slowly, or risks pinch-flatting.

Working backwards from a target drop gives you a pressure. That pressure depends on two things above all: how much weight the tyre carries and how wide it is. Width matters enormously, because a wider casing has more air volume and a larger contact patch, so it needs far less pressure to carry the same load.

This approach rests on the published tyre-drop research of Frank Berto, whose load-versus-pressure measurements are the foundation most modern pressure guidance is built on.

Step 1 — split the load

Your system weight — rider, bike, kit, bottles, luggage — is divided between the wheels according to the weight distribution you set. A road bike typically carries 45–48% on the front; a time trial or track bike more; a mountain bike less. Front and rear are then calculated independently, which is why they come out different, and why you can run different tyres at each end.

Step 2 — the base pressure

Each wheel's load and tyre width produce a base pressure. Pressure falls with the square of casing width, which is why going from 25 mm to 32 mm changes the answer so much more than most riders expect, plus a smaller linear term that keeps very narrow tyres realistic.

Step 3 — the modifiers

The base figure is then adjusted for what you are actually riding.

Casing and tube

A supple casing with less internal friction needs less pressure for the same feel; tubeless setups can run lower still because there is no tube to pinch.

Step 4 — safety limits

The result is held between 18 psi and a ceiling that depends on your setup: 72 psi for tubeless, which is the usual maximum for hookless tubeless rims, and 140 psi otherwise.

When a cap changes the answer, the app says so and shows you the uncapped figure as well. A capped number presented on its own would be misleading — you should know when the model wanted more pressure than your equipment allows, so you can check your own rim and tyre limits and decide.

Where this model stops being valid

Above roughly 65 mm, treat the number as an upper bound. The width term that keeps narrow tyres honest over-estimates for very wide casings, so fat bikes and 3-inch plus tyres will read high. Widening the model's valid range is on the roadmap.

Two further honest caveats:

  • Casings vary between makes. The model uses width, not the specific model, so two 32 mm tyres with very different casings get the same recommendation. In practice a supple, high-thread-count casing will feel good a few psi lower than a stiff one.
  • Temperature is not accounted for. Pressure rises with heat. A tyre inflated in a cold garage will read higher after an hour in the sun.

Use a gauge

These are starting points, not guarantees. Set the pressure with an accurate gauge, ride it, and adjust by two or three psi at a time until it feels right — your judgement over a few rides beats any calculator. Never exceed the maximum printed on your tyre or specified by your rim manufacturer. On hookless rims in particular, over-inflation can unseat a tyre at speed.
